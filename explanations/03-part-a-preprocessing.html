In the notebooks · where the 500 validation articles went

The B1 run and the T5 fine-tuning in C1 hold out the last 500 training rows (df2[:-500] and [-500:]). The four B2 runs build their data from the full df2, so they train on all 13,879 articles. None of the runs touch test.csv during training, so the test scores remain fair.

What each cleaning step does, and why

Stopword removal

Words like "the", "of", "is", "over" appear in nearly every article, so they say almost nothing about what one specific article is about. Removing them shortens every sequence (fewer recurrent steps, and less distance for the gradient to travel, per page 2 section 8). It also stops frequent filler words from taking up vocabulary slots.

Stemming vs. lemmatization

Both reduce different forms of a word to one shared form, so "reef" and "reefs" share a single embedding row instead of splitting the training signal between two. They do it differently:

WordStemming (Porter, rule-based suffix chopping)Lemmatization (dictionary + part of speech)
studiesstudistudy
largestlargestlarge
composedcomposcompose

Stemming can produce non-words, and lemmatization produces real dictionary forms. This project uses lemmatization. That matters in Part B2: GloVe's pretrained vocabulary contains "study" but not "studi".

Punctuation, numbers and symbols

Only purely alphabetic tokens are kept, which removes punctuation, numbers like "2,900" and mixed tokens like "'s".

The exact rule the code applies

For every token spaCy produces, the notebook keeps the lowercase lemma if and only if the token is not a stopword, not punctuation, and purely alphabetic:

\[ \text{keep}(\tau) = \neg\,\texttt{is\_stop}(\tau) \;\wedge\; \neg\,\texttt{is\_punct}(\tau) \;\wedge\; \texttt{is\_alpha}(\tau), \qquad \text{output} = \texttt{lower}\big(\texttt{lemma}(\tau)\big) \]

\(\neg\) is "not" and \(\wedge\) is "and". The kept lemmas are joined back together with spaces. This same rule is applied to article texts and to titles, in both the training and test files.

In the notebooks · three consequences worth knowing
  • The RNN's target titles are cleaned too. Part B learns to produce lowercase lemma sequences like "great barrier reef", and its ROUGE scores compare against those cleaned titles. Parts C1 and C2 use the raw text and raw titles instead, as the spec requires. So Part B and Part C scores aren't measured against identical references.
  • Sentence boundaries are gone. Removing punctuation deletes the periods that separate sentences, so the cleaned text is one continuous stream of words. This matters for Part B2's hierarchical encoder, which is specified to work sentence by sentence.
  • Non-ASCII letters survive. is_alpha is true for accented letters, so a word like "café" is kept. Spec step 2's non-ASCII removal is only partly covered by this filter.

Why spaCy, and why batching made it fast

docs/report.pdf
"I had tried multiple libraries including NLTK, spacy and their various methods for stopwords removal and lemmatization. But as spaCy is designed for large-scale NLP tasks and is significantly faster than NLTK due to its Cython-based implementation, I choose to do the cleaning through it. […] By implementing batch processing, I have significantly improved the preprocessing time […] which would otherwise was taking around an hour to do so."

spaCy's lemmatizer needs each word's part of speech ("composed" used as a verb lemmatizes to "compose"), so it runs a small statistical model over the text. Two choices keep that affordable:

  • Only what's needed runs. The dependency parser and named-entity recognizer aren't used, so they're disabled when loading en_core_web_sm.
  • Documents are processed in batches. nlp.pipe(texts, batch_size=50) sends 50 documents at a time through the pipeline. Its model computations run as one grouped operation per batch rather than 50 separate calls, which spreads the fixed cost of each call over many documents.
\[ \text{throughput} = 14.62 \text{ articles/s} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{13{,}879}{14.62} \approx 949 \text{ s} \approx 15 \text{ min } 49 \text{ s} \]

The rate and total time come from the notebook's progress bar. Against the report's roughly one-hour figure for the unbatched approach, that's close to a 3.8× speed-up (\(60 / 15.8 \approx 3.8\)).
